Keeping the Farm in the Family

Ag dedication on Hawaiʻi Island: the County tax program that can cut a farm's land assessment to a fraction of market value, what changed in 2026, and why heirs can't afford to let it lapse.

← All Journal posts
Legacy November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Families here rarely lose land all at once. More often it happens slowly: the land is worth more on paper every year, the tax bill grows with it, and one day the next generation can't keep up.

I grew up on our family's ranch land in Kona, and I've helped manage family farmland most of my life. One of the quiet tools that keeps working land in working hands is the County's agricultural dedication. It doesn't make headlines, and it's easy to forget about until the tax bill arrives.

If your family holds ag-zoned land on this island, or you're about to inherit some, this is worth ten minutes. The rules changed recently, and 2026 has two deadlines you should know about.

The BasicsWhat Ag Dedication Is, in Plain Words

Ag dedication is a promise. You agree to keep farming or ranching your land for a set number of years, and in return the County taxes the land on its value as a farm, not on what a developer or buyer would pay for it.

That gap can be huge. One Big Island owner writing for Hawaiʻi Life in 2024 reported that the County assessed their land at about $480,000, but its dedicated agricultural use value was only about $25,000 (Hawaiʻi Life). Your numbers will differ, but the scale of the savings is why this program matters to families.

What changed. In 2023 and 2024, the County Council tightened the rules to stop landowners with little real farming from claiming the break. The new programs took effect September 2, 2024, and owners now have to show actual, ongoing agricultural use. The old non-dedicated program is being phased out: anyone still in it had until September 1, 2026 to apply for one of the new programs to keep their ag tax status (County ag program FAQ).

The ProgramsWho Qualifies and How to Apply

Hawaiʻi County now offers three agricultural programs. The longer you commit, the bigger the break (County ag program FAQ).

ProgramCommitmentLand assessed atBest for
10-year dedication10 years1× ag use valueEstablished farms and ranches the family plans to keep; lowest assessment
3-year dedication3 years2× ag use valueFamilies still deciding the land's long-term future
Community Food SustainabilityRenews every 5 years30% of market valueSmaller food-growing operations

The basics to qualify (County application form):

  • The parcel must have County agricultural zoning. A garden on a residential lot doesn't count.
  • The application asks whether the farm grosses at least $2,000 a year. If it does, you attach your prior-year GET form G-49 or IRS Schedule F as proof. If not, you include a farm plan.
  • You need one supporting document: a farm plan (the County has a template), an NRCS conservation plan, USDA organic or food safety certification, at least $10,000 in farm expense or investment receipts within two years, or an agricultural conservation easement.
  • You'll sketch the farm area, homesite and buildings on a map, and the County may inspect.
Deadline · extended this year

The normal deadline is September 1, and an approved dedication takes effect the following July 1. For 2026 only, the County has extended the filing deadline to December 14, 2026, because of Tropical Storm Lala (County Real Property Tax). If you missed September, you still have time this year.

Applications can be mailed, emailed, or dropped off at the Real Property Tax offices in Hilo (Aupuni Center) and Kona (West Hawaiʻi Civic Center). The office's general email is cohrpt@hawaiicounty.gov.

The Legacy PartWhen Land Changes Hands

Here's what families miss. A dedication is a commitment that comes with the land, not just with the person who signed it.

Rollback taxes. If the qualifying farm use stops, the use changes, or the dedication's conditions are broken, the County can charge rollback taxes: the difference between the farm assessment and the full market assessment, reaching back over past years, plus possible penalties (Living on Big Island). An heir who inherits a 10-year dedication and lets the coffee go wild may inherit that bill too.

Selling. A long-term dedication generally stays with the property after a sale unless the County releases it, so the buyer takes on both the farming commitment and the rollback risk (Living on Big Island). Before buying ag land, check the tax assessment and the title report for an existing dedication.

Leaving the program. When land comes out of an ag program, its assessment can jump straight to market value. In 2026 the Council created some relief for older farmers: under the new Agricultural Kūpuna Program, a principal farmer or rancher who is 65 or older, has been in an ag program for 10 of the last 15 years, and receives the homeowner exemption can keep the lower ag value, with increases capped at 3% a year. No new conversions are allowed after December 31, 2029 (County info sheet).

New This Year: ʻĀina Kūpuna

For families on very old family land, the County has a brand-new program. ʻĀina Kūpuna (ancestral land) lets qualifying families dedicate their land for 10 years and pay only the County's minimum tax, currently $200 a year (County Real Property Tax).

Under the final ordinance, the property must:

  • be less than 20 acres
  • be owned, in whole or in part, by a descendant of someone who owned it before January 1, 1926
  • have carried more than $10,000 in property taxes over the most recent 10 years (or would have, without an ag assessment)
  • have at least one owner living in Hawaiʻi County

During the dedication, the land can't be sold or transferred to someone outside the line of descent, and it can't be used commercially or as a short-term rental (under 180 days). Each person can claim only one ʻĀina Kūpuna property, and breaking the dedication means repaying the tax savings plus a 10% penalty. Families may need deeds, wills, trusts, birth and death certificates, or OHA genealogy verification to prove descent (County info sheet and application).

Deadline · first year

The first-year deadline is December 31, 2026. After that it moves to September 1 each year (County Real Property Tax). The County's info sheet and application are on that page.

Your Next StepsA Checklist for Families and Heirs

  • Look up the parcel on the County's property tax site and note its current assessment and program, if any.
  • Check the title report for an existing dedication and its end date.
  • If you're farming but not dedicated, file by December 14, 2026 this year (September 1 in normal years).
  • If the land has been in the family since before 1926, look into ʻĀina Kūpuna before December 31, 2026.
  • Keep farm records year-round: receipts, GET returns, photos, a simple farm plan.
  • Before selling, subdividing, or changing how the land is used, ask what it means for rollback taxes.
  • Make sure whoever inherits knows the land is dedicated and what that commits them to.

Our family has been on this land a long time, and I've learned that keeping it is about paperwork as much as aloha. Ag dedication is one of the most practical tools a farming family has.
